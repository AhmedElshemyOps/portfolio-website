1. Your team has enough capacity. So why is everyone overwhelmed?

It is 8:30 a.m. at a destination management company.

Three airport transfer groups need confirmation. Two excursion departures are approaching. A hotel requests a revised guest manifest, and a transport supplier is waiting for updated instructions.

The operations team is busy, but the problem is not necessarily the total number of tasks.

The problem may be when those tasks arrive, when they are released for processing, and how different types of work compete for the same resources.

At 8:00 a.m., the team may have spare capacity.

At 9:00 a.m., everyone may be overloaded.

By 11:00 a.m., the pressure has disappeared.

Adding employees for the entire morning might solve one bottleneck while creating unused capacity elsewhere.

Lean Management offers another way to investigate this pattern.

It is called Heijunka (平準化).

Heijunka is not about forcing every hour to look identical. It is about creating a more predictable operating rhythm wherever the work can safely be planned, prepared, sequenced or released differently.

For tourism businesses, the challenge is deciding which activities can be leveled and which must happen at a fixed time.

2. What is Heijunka?

Heijunka is a Japanese Lean Management concept commonly translated as production leveling or production smoothing.

In manufacturing, it aims to distribute production quantity and product mix more evenly across a defined planning period.

Instead of producing large batches of one product and then switching to another, a manufacturer may use smaller, mixed sequences aligned with demand.

The objective is a more stable operating system with fewer disruptive peaks and less unevenness.

The Lean Enterprise Institute identifies leveling quantity and type of production as central to Heijunka. Toyota also describes its role in managing production volume and the sequencing of different vehicle specifications.

Two dimensions of Heijunka

Volume leveling means smoothing the amount of work released into a process over time.

Mix leveling means balancing the different categories of work so that one difficult task type does not dominate an entire period.

In a tourism company, the equivalent categories might be:

  • Airport transfer preparation
  • Excursion manifest verification
  • Hotel group arrival documentation
  • Supplier confirmations
  • Guest special-request validation

A schedule that releases 15 complex cases together may be much harder to manage than one that distributes those cases among simpler tasks.

However, the service sector introduces a critical limitation.

You cannot level a flight's actual arrival time, a guest's contractual pickup time or a conference's scheduled opening simply because your staffing chart looks better.

Instead, you level the eligible preparation work surrounding those events.

3. Heijunka is not Yamazumi

These two Lean tools are related, but they answer different questions.

DimensionYamazumiHeijunka
Primary questionWho has how much work?When and in what sequence should eligible work be released?
Main visualStacked workload columnsTime-based leveling board or schedule
Main concernWorkload imbalanceDemand variation and uneven work release
Tourism applicationCompare coordinator workloadSchedule advance preparation across available periods
Key limitationDoes not automatically solve schedulingCannot move immovable guest commitments

A Yamazumi chart may reveal that one coordinator has 68 minutes of work within a 60-minute window.

Heijunka may help determine whether some of that work could have been completed earlier, released in a different sequence or shared through a controlled preparation process.

The strongest improvement may use both tools together.

4. Understanding Muda, Mura and Muri

Mura — Unevenness

A team alternates between quiet periods and sudden workload spikes.

Muri — Overburden

People or systems are asked to perform more work than their safe, sustainable capacity allows.

Muda — Waste

The process contains activities that consume resources without delivering necessary value, such as avoidable rework or repeated data entry.

These concepts interact.

Uneven work release can create overburden. Overburden can contribute to mistakes. Mistakes may create rework, which adds further workload.

But managers should not assume every busy period represents waste. Some peaks are real and unavoidable.

The purpose of analysis is to distinguish preventable variation from genuine demand.

5. Case study: A DMC facing a morning preparation peak

Imagine a DMC coordinating airport transfers, excursions and hotel arrivals.

The company has two trained operations employees responsible for a defined category of pre-departure preparation tasks.

The morning preparation window runs from 8:00 a.m. until 11:00 a.m.

For teaching purposes, assume:

  • Each preparation package requires six minutes of active work.
  • Each employee provides 30 minutes of nominal processing capacity per half-hour.
  • Combined nominal capacity is 60 minutes per half-hour.
  • There are 44 preparation packages across six half-hour periods.
  • The preparation packages have different deadlines, but some can be completed earlier because the required information is already available.

The initial release pattern is:

Total44264360

The 9:00–9:30 period contains 72 minutes of assigned work against 60 minutes of nominal capacity.

That is a 12-minute capacity deficit for the period, before considering interruptions, breaks or variation in actual processing times.

Across the entire morning, the team has 360 minutes of nominal capacity and only 264 minutes of assigned preparation work.

Average nominal utilization is:

264 ÷ 360 × 100 = 73.3%

The average suggests sufficient capacity.

The peak period tells a different story.

What is actually going wrong?

The problem is not necessarily insufficient total staffing.

It may be that the company releases work according to when someone notices it, rather than according to a controlled preparation plan.

Some work is unnecessarily delayed until the departure becomes urgent.

Other tasks arrive in large batches from different departments.

The team spends the morning reacting to the release pattern instead of following a stable preparation sequence.

This is a candidate for Heijunka analysis.

6. Designing a safer future state

Suppose the team reviews the 44 packages and confirms that several preparation packages can be safely reassigned across available time windows: four from 9:00 a.m. to 8:00 a.m., two from 9:30 a.m. to 10:00 a.m., and two from 9:30 a.m. to 10:30 a.m.

The relevant information is already verified, and the preparation remains valid until departure.

A revised illustrative release schedule could be:

PeriodCurrent releaseProposed release
08:00–08:3048
08:30–09:0088
09:00–09:30128
09:30–10:00108
10:00–10:3068
10:30–11:0044
Total4444

The total workload does not change.

Only the timing of eligible preparation work changes.

Under the proposed release pattern, the highest half-hour workload becomes:

8 packages × 6 minutes = 48 minutes

The maximum planned workload falls from 72 to 48 minutes per half-hour.

This represents a reduction of:

(72 − 48) ÷ 72 × 100 = 33.3% in peak released workload

It is not a 33.3% reduction in total labor hours, payroll expense or customer demand.

The team still performs 264 minutes of preparation work.

The potential benefit is a less concentrated workload and more available capacity during the original peak.

Important feasibility conditions

  1. The reassigned packages can genuinely be prepared in their proposed time windows.
  2. Guest and supplier information is available and sufficiently reliable.
  3. Earlier preparation does not create unacceptable stale-data risk.
  4. All packages still meet their individual deadlines.
  5. Staff have the necessary skills and access rights.
  6. Earlier work does not displace more urgent commitments.
  7. A controlled revalidation step is retained when circumstances change.

If these conditions fail, the schedule must be redesigned.

A visually balanced chart is not proof of a feasible operating plan.

8. How to build a Heijunka box for tourism operations

A traditional Heijunka box is a visual planning system that organizes work by type and fixed time interval.

In manufacturing, cards can represent production instructions issued at regular intervals.

A tourism adaptation might use rows for work categories and columns for half-hour preparation periods.

Example: Tourism operations leveling board

Work category08:0008:3009:0009:30
Transfer preparation2222
Excursion manifests2222
Hotel group documentation1111
Supplier validation1111

This is a conceptual planning board, not the complete 44-package case-study schedule.

Each card should contain a reference ID, deadline, responsible role, work category, readiness status and escalation condition.

Avoid displaying unnecessary guest personal information on a shared board.

The difference between planning and dispatch

Advance preparation: Tasks that may be scheduled earlier when information and authorization are available.

Deadline-controlled work: Tasks that must be completed within a defined window.

Real-time response: Activities such as responding to a flight disruption or guest emergency.

Only the first category is a straightforward candidate for advance leveling.

The second requires careful deadline-based planning.

The third requires reserved response capacity rather than artificial smoothing.

9. Volume leveling versus mix leveling

A DMC may process the same number of preparation packages every half-hour and still experience uneven workload.

Why?

Because not all packages require the same amount of work.

For example:

Package typeIllustrative standard time
Standard transfer confirmation4 minutes
Excursion manifest verification6 minutes
Complex group movement review12 minutes

A period containing six standard confirmations requires approximately 24 minutes.

A period containing six complex group reviews requires approximately 72 minutes.

The number of packages is identical, but the labor content is very different.

This is why a mature Heijunka approach should consider work content and mix, not simply task counts.

Managers can estimate weighted workload as:

Planned work minutes = Σ (package quantity × validated standard processing time)

The standard times must be established from representative observations and updated when the process changes.

10. What about unpredictable flight arrivals?

A flight may arrive late, early or at a different terminal.

A tour may encounter traffic disruption.

A conference speaker may miss a connection.

These are genuine operational uncertainties.

Heijunka cannot remove them.

The objective is to reduce avoidable preparation peaks so that teams retain enough flexibility to respond when unexpected events occur.

  • Reserving explicit response capacity for disruptions.
  • Separating confirmed tasks from tasks waiting for external information.
  • Establishing clear deadlines for supplier confirmations.
  • Using flight-status monitoring with a human escalation owner.
  • Revalidating documents when guest or transport information changes.
  • Escalating predicted overload before it becomes a missed service commitment.

A perfectly flat utilization chart can be dangerous if it leaves no capacity for variation.

11. Hotel application: Group check-in preparation

Consider a hotel expecting three groups during the afternoon.

The arrival times are fixed by the guests' travel arrangements.

The hotel should not ask everyone to arrive earlier merely to improve the staff utilization chart.

Instead, it can investigate which preparatory activities are eligible for earlier completion.

Examples include room-block verification, welcome-material preparation, approved billing checks and group coordinator communication.

The front desk can use a visual board to track preparation readiness.

The board should separate:

  • Ready and verified
  • Awaiting information
  • Requires revalidation
  • Escalation required

Room assignment and personal data must remain within authorized hotel systems.

The hotel should also preserve appropriate arrival-time staffing and guest-service capacity.

The goal is to reduce last-minute preparation pressure, not move work so early that information becomes unreliable.

12. MICE application: Conference registration and supplier readiness

A conference may have a fixed opening time, a scheduled keynote and several simultaneous supplier activities.

These cannot all be shifted.

But eligible preparatory work may include badge printing, approved attendee-list reconciliation, equipment testing, signage preparation and supplier confirmation.

A leveling plan can distribute preparation over earlier periods while preserving final validation gates.

For example, badges might be prepared before the event, but an updated attendee list could require controlled corrections.

The event manager must decide what can be finalized, what remains provisional and what must be checked immediately before use.

This distinction is essential in applying manufacturing concepts to service operations.

13. Calculating whether Heijunka creates financial value

In the fictional DMC example, total preparation time remains 264 minutes.

Therefore:

Direct labor minutes saved = 0

It would be misleading to claim an 18-minute or 24-minute labor saving simply because the peak was reduced.

The potential financial benefits, if measured, may come from fewer overtime events, reduced avoidable rework, fewer missed deadlines or more reliable service delivery.

For example, assume a pilot later documents a reduction of 20 overtime minutes per operating day, with 20 comparable operating days per month.

20 × 20 = 400 overtime minutes.

400 ÷ 60 = 6.67 hours.

At an illustrative incremental overtime cost of €15 per hour:

6.67 × €15 = approximately €100 per month.

This would be an estimated avoided incremental overtime cost only if the overtime reduction were actually observed, attributable and reflected in paid hours.

It is not a result demonstrated by the case study.

Never convert theoretical capacity into claimed financial savings without evidence.

14. Seven-day Heijunka pilot

Day 1 — Define the service boundary

Choose one repeatable workflow, such as pre-departure preparation for airport transfers. Record guest commitments, operational deadlines and non-negotiable safety controls.

Day 2 — Collect task data

Capture arrival times, release times, processing times, readiness conditions, rework, interruptions and actual completion deadlines. Use representative observations rather than estimates alone.

Day 3 — Visualize the current state

Create vertical workload charts by time interval. Separate genuine demand spikes from internally created batching and delayed work release.

Day 4 — Design a future-state sequence

Identify tasks eligible for earlier preparation. Create a leveling board and validate capacity, task dependencies, skills and deadlines.

Day 5 — Conduct a controlled pilot

Test the new release schedule on a limited, low-risk workflow. Keep the previous process available as a fallback.

Day 6 — Review results

Compare peak workload, late completion, rework, overtime, escalations and guest-service outcomes.

Day 7 — Decide

Adopt, revise or reject the proposed change based on evidence. Update standard work only after the process owner approves the results.

16. Using AI to support Heijunka

AI can help structure task data, calculate workload by interval, identify potential release alternatives and explain operational trade-offs.

It can also generate draft charts, suggest a pilot design and flag missing assumptions.

However, AI cannot determine from a spreadsheet alone whether a guest manifest is safe to finalize early, whether a supplier agreement permits a change or whether staff are trained to perform a task.

Those decisions require authorized operational review.

The following prompts are designed to support analysis, not automatically implement operational changes.

17. Common mistakes when implementing Heijunka

Mistake 1: Trying to flatten real guest demand. Actual flight arrivals and event schedules are constraints, not simply scheduling preferences.

Mistake 2: Measuring task counts without processing time. Ten complex tasks may require far more work than ten simple ones.

Mistake 3: Moving preparation too early. Information may change, creating rework or service risk.

Mistake 4: Planning at 100% capacity. A schedule without interruption or response capacity is fragile.

Mistake 5: Claiming savings that were never measured. Redistributed work is not automatically eliminated work.

Mistake 6: Treating AI output as an approved operating procedure. Human validation and management authorization remain necessary.

Mistake 7: Using a leveling board without daily management. A board is only useful when its information is accurate, current and acted upon.

18. Frequently asked questions

Is Heijunka suitable for service industries?

Yes, as an adaptation of the manufacturing concept, particularly where work can be prepared, sequenced or released within controlled time windows. It is less directly applicable to unpredictable, immediate guest-facing demand.

Is Heijunka the same as staff scheduling?

No. Staff scheduling determines who is available. Heijunka focuses on the volume, mix and timing of work released into a process. Both need to be coordinated.

Can Heijunka reduce staffing costs?

Possibly, if a measured implementation reduces actual overtime, rework or other costs. A smoother chart alone does not establish financial savings.

What is the difference between Heijunka and Kanban?

Heijunka concerns leveling the production or work-release pattern. Kanban is a signaling mechanism commonly used to control replenishment or workflow. A Heijunka box can use Kanban cards.

Can AI create a Heijunka schedule?

AI can propose a schedule from supplied constraints, but it must be checked against actual task dependencies, information availability, staffing capability and service commitments.

Does Heijunka eliminate demand peaks?

No. It can reduce avoidable variation in eligible work release, but it cannot eliminate all real-world demand variation.

Conclusion: Level the work you can control

Tourism operations will always face uncertainty.

Flights will be delayed. Guests will arrive in waves. Events will run against fixed schedules, and suppliers will occasionally fail.

Heijunka does not make those realities disappear.

What it offers is a disciplined way to ask:

Are we overloaded because customer demand is genuinely unavoidable—or because our own operating system releases too much work at once?

That question can reveal opportunities to prepare earlier, sequence work more intelligently and preserve capacity for the unexpected.

For tourism leaders, the objective is not simply a smoother schedule.

It is a more reliable service operation that protects guests, employees and essential controls.
