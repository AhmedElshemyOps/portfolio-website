#SOP design starts with the process, not the page

The easiest mistake in SOP work is to open a blank template and start writing steps. Professional process design begins earlier.

Before a procedure is drafted, management should understand the purpose of the process, its trigger, required inputs, intended output, real roles, systems, control points, records, normal completion condition, exceptions and decision authority.

ISO's guidance on the process approach describes processes as interrelated activities that use inputs to deliver intended results, supported by PDCA and risk-based thinking. ISO 10013:2021 separately provides guidance on developing and maintaining documented information that supports effective management-system processes. Those ideas fit hotel apartment operations well: the procedure should support the process, not become a substitute for understanding it.

A hotel apartment should therefore avoid asking AI to write an SOP before the current operating reality is known. A better sequence is: observe, map, validate, then draft.

#The SOP-design HOTEL model

H — Hospitality role & human owner: Who owns the process, performs it, approves the SOP and authorizes exceptions?

O — Operational objective & context: What outcome must the process achieve, in which property, department, shift and guest-journey stage?

T — Trusted inputs & sources of truth: Which current SOPs, policies, PMS/CMMS/RMS records, forms, contracts or controlled standards define the process?

E — Expected output, exceptions & evidence: What document structure is required, which records prove critical steps and what happens when the normal path fails?

L — Limits, privacy & leadership approval: What may AI not decide, which data must stay protected and who validates the process before publication?

For SOP work, every important instruction should connect to a role, decision, system or record. If it connects to none of them, management should challenge whether it is useful, observable or auditable.

#Fictional UAE case: redesigning the room-not-ready process

Illustrative scenario

Operational teaching scenario, not a documented incident. Uncited figures and outcomes are illustrative assumptions.

Situation

Continue with the fictional 60-unit UAE serviced-apartment portfolio.

Evidence

Management sees recurring friction when an apartment is not ready at the expected arrival time. The existing SOP says: “If the room is not ready, inform the guest and contact housekeeping.”

That sentence leaves the important controls unanswered: how “not ready” is defined, which system owns status, who confirms the next realistic readiness time, what happens if Engineering is the blocker, how often the guest is updated, what Front Office may promise, when the Duty Manager becomes involved, what compensation authority exists and which record proves closure.

A redesigned process separates cleaning in progress, inspection pending, technical blocker and management exception. AI can expose missing controls. The hotel apartment team must still decide the real roles, thresholds and authority.

Check approved source records, data boundaries, the task requirements and the named owner who can verify the output.

Decision

Decide whether the inputs are sufficient and the use is authorised; stop when a required fact or approval is missing.

Action

Prepare a draft that separates confirmed facts, assumptions, unresolved items and recommendations for human review.

Verification

Check every operational claim against its source and confirm the named owner’s approval before using the draft in live work.

#Process discovery should expose hidden work

Formal procedures often describe what management thinks happens. Actual work may include undocumented calls, WhatsApp messages, handwritten lists, verbal approvals, workarounds, duplicate system entry and unofficial escalation.

Before redesigning an SOP, observe and interview the people who perform the process. Identify what starts the work, which information is usually missing, where the team leaves the system, what happens when the normal case fails, which decisions need approval and which record proves completion.

The purpose is not to legitimize every workaround. It is to make the current state visible before management designs the future state.

#Build the SOP architecture before drafting paragraphs

Prompt 111 creates the document architecture. A controlled hotel apartment SOP can include document identifier, owner and approver, purpose, scope, definitions, roles and authority, triggers and prerequisites, procedure steps, decision points, exceptions and escalation, records/evidence, KPIs, training, references, revision history and review date.

Risk should determine the amount of detail. Policy, SOP, work instruction and checklist are different document levels and should not be merged accidentally.

#A procedure step should be executable and auditable

Prompt 112 converts the validated process into executable steps. Strong steps identify the role, action, trusted source, decision condition, evidence and escalation behavior.

A useful procedure table can contain Step | Role | Action | System/source | Decision/condition | Evidence | Escalation. This is more operationally useful than a long narrative paragraph.

#RACI should clarify authority, not decorate the SOP

Prompt 113 builds a RACI only after the actual process roles are known. Responsible performs the work, Accountable owns the outcome, Consulted contributes before the decision and Informed receives the result/status.

Avoid multiple Accountables for the same decision and do not infer compensation, financial, safety, pricing or employment authority from a job title. Link the SOP to the approved delegation framework where necessary.

#Exception design is where professional SOPs become operational

Prompt 114 controls the moment when the normal process cannot continue. A useful exception matrix identifies the exception, detection method, immediate containment, owner, escalation trigger, authorized options and evidence required for closure.

“In case of any issue, contact the manager” is not an exception process.

#Evidence design turns an SOP into a controllable system

Prompt 115 asks one question for every critical instruction: How will we know this step happened correctly?

Evidence can be a PMS status change, inspection checklist, work-order closeout, timestamped communication, approval record, controlled form, system transaction, inventory count or training acknowledgement.

Do not create records merely to create paperwork. Evidence should support control, traceability, service recovery, compliance or improvement.

#KPIs should test the process outcome, not reward shortcuts

Prompt 116 creates measures only after the objective and controls are clear. Every KPI needs a definition, denominator, source, owner, review frequency and anti-gaming guardrail.

AI may propose indicators. Management approves targets.

#Work instructions and checklists should stay connected to the SOP

Prompt 117 creates detailed task instructions and Prompt 118 converts approved requirements into a practical checklist.

A useful hierarchy is Policy → SOP → Work instruction → Checklist/form. If the SOP changes, dependent documents may also require revision.

#SOP audits should compare controlled design with actual practice

Prompt 119 compares the approved SOP with observed work, system records, incidents, complaints, employee feedback, linked policies, current technology and the current authority model.

The aim is to determine whether the document still controls the process effectively—not whether every employee can recite its wording.

#Revision control prevents a correct SOP from becoming a new risk

Prompt 120 reviews the impact of a proposed change before publication. A revision can affect roles, approval authority, system fields, forms, training, KPIs, vendors, guest communication, checklists, audit criteria, linked SOPs and automation.

Revision impact should therefore be assessed before the old document is withdrawn.

#Lean Six Sigma can strengthen SOP design

Define the problem and process boundary. Measure the current process, timing, defects and rework. Analyze verified causes. Improve the process and controls. Control through SOPs, work instructions, training, measurement and audit.

The SOP should document a stabilized improved process, not freeze an inefficient current state simply because it is familiar.

#SOP governance dashboard

Useful governance measures include SOP review completion, overdue controlled documents, exception frequency per process volume, repeat SOP-related finding rate, training completion, field-validation pass rate, undocumented-workaround count, obsolete-reference rate, overdue revision-impact actions and post-publication rework.

The dashboard should distinguish document completion from process effectiveness.

#Illustrative Before → Action → After → AED Saved

Illustrative example only: 18 duplicated coordination contacts per month × 6 avoidable minutes = 108 minutes, or 1.8 hours. At an illustrative AED 45/hour internal labor value, that equals AED 81/month.

After redesigning the process around one status owner, verified dependency field, update interval and escalation threshold, duplicated contacts fall from 18 to 6. Avoided time is 72 minutes, or 1.2 hours: AED 54/month illustrative direct labor avoidance.

Real business cases should use Finance-approved values, comparable periods and verified counts.

#30-day implementation plan

Days 1–5: inventory high-risk/high-friction procedures and confirm owners.

Days 6–10: observe and map the current process, systems, evidence and exception paths.

Days 11–15: draft and challenge structure, RACI, authority, evidence and escalation.

Days 16–20: define KPIs, work instructions, checklists and training.

Days 21–25: pilot on a controlled scope and record confusion, workarounds and exceptions.

Days 26–30: approve the version, withdraw obsolete copies, train roles and schedule the first audit/review.

#Management checklist

Before release, confirm the process was validated; purpose/scope are clear; inputs and outputs are defined; critical decisions have one accountable owner; authority matches delegation; systems are current; normal and exception paths are explicit; evidence is practical; privacy is protected; KPIs have denominators; dependent documents are linked; training is defined; field testing is complete; and obsolete versions can be withdrawn.
