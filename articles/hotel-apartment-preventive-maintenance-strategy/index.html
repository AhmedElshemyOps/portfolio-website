Preventive Maintenance Strategy for Hotel Apartments

Preventive maintenance is not a calendar of engineering tasks. It is a reliability system designed to reduce the probability that an asset failure becomes an apartment outage, a guest complaint, an emergency purchase or an avoidable operating cost.

Direct answer

Preventive maintenance should be risk-based, not calendar-based

The objective is not to complete the largest possible number of PM tasks. The objective is to apply the right maintenance intervention to the right asset at the right interval so that reliability improves at an economically and operationally sensible cost.

A strong PM system connects asset register → criticality → maintenance requirement → frequency → work order → execution evidence → defect history → KPI review → frequency/control adjustment.

The management problem

The reactive-maintenance trap

Reactive work feels productive because the defect is visible and the response is immediate. But a property dominated by emergency calls can spend engineering capacity repeatedly restoring service without reducing the conditions creating the failures.

A leaking flexible hose, blocked fan-coil filter or deteriorating door closer may begin as a small technical condition. If it is discovered only after failure, the operational consequence can include an unavailable apartment, guest relocation, overtime, emergency procurement, housekeeping rework and service recovery.

Control question

For every recurring failure, ask: was the asset missing from the PM program, was the task ineffective, was the frequency wrong, was the work not completed, or was the completed work not verified?

Step 1 · Prioritize

Start with asset criticality

Not every asset deserves the same maintenance effort. Criticality should reflect the consequence of failure rather than the purchase price alone.

A practical property-level assessment can score four dimensions from 1 to 5: guest impact, safety/compliance impact, lost-capacity or revenue impact, and difficulty/time to recover. The scoring method is an internal management design—not a universal industry benchmark—and should be documented and applied consistently.

Illustrative method: a domestic kettle might score low because replacement is fast and localized. A main domestic-water booster pump, central access-control component or apartment HVAC serving a high-occupancy unit may score much higher because failure can affect service availability or several guests.

Step 2 · Design

Set PM frequency from evidence

Intervals should consider manufacturer requirements, warranty conditions, statutory requirements where applicable, asset condition, operating hours, environmental exposure, failure history and the consequence of failure. A monthly interval is not automatically better than quarterly; over-maintenance can waste labor and introduce its own errors.

Start from approved requirements and known risk, then review actual failure and inspection evidence. If a monthly inspection repeatedly finds no deterioration, the interval may deserve review. If defects repeatedly appear between visits, the task content or frequency may be inadequate.

Step 3 · Execute

A PM is complete only when evidence supports closure

A scheduled work order should identify the asset, task, standard, due date, responsible role, required readings or checks, defects found, parts used where relevant, completion time and verifier where verification is required.

“Done” is not evidence. A meaningful closure record should allow another competent person to understand what was inspected or serviced, what condition was found and what follow-up remains.

Step 4 · Control

Separate PM compliance from maintenance backlog

A team can report high PM completion while defects discovered during PM remain unresolved. Track scheduled PM and corrective backlog separately, then connect them through the originating work order.

Backlog should be aged and prioritized. Critical overdue work requires escalation based on consequence and approved authority—not simply a red color on a dashboard.

Step 5 · Measure

Measure effectiveness, not activity alone

Useful measures include PM compliance %, overdue critical PM count, corrective work generated from PM, repeat-failure rate, emergency-work share, backlog age, apartment downtime attributable to technical defects, first-time-fix %, and—where definitions and data are reliable—MTBF and MTTR.

Formula example: if 184 PM work orders were due during a month and 173 were completed within the approved due window, PM compliance = 173 ÷ 184 × 100 = 94.0%. That number says nothing by itself about effectiveness; management still needs to inspect failures, repeat defects and overdue critical work.

Worked example · Illustrative, not a benchmark

A 60-apartment serviced property: what changes when PM becomes risk-based?

Assume a fictional 60-apartment property with 60 fan-coil units serving the main living/sleeping areas. During the previous 90 days, engineering recorded 18 guest-facing HVAC defect calls. Six were repeat calls on apartments that had already received a reactive repair.

For teaching purposes, assume each guest-facing HVAC incident consumes an average of 1.5 engineering labor hours, 0.5 operations/guest-service hour and AED 85 in average parts/consumables. These are scenario assumptions, not industry averages.

Visible 90-day workload = 18 × (1.5 + 0.5) = 36 labor hours. Visible parts/consumables = 18 × AED 85 = AED 1,530. This still excludes lost apartment capacity, guest compensation, overtime, vendor call-outs and reputation impact.

The team reviews the failure history and finds that 12 of the 18 calls involve airflow restriction, condensate drainage or conditions detectable during a competent inspection. Management does not conclude that “PM will eliminate 12 failures.” Instead, it changes the control system: asset IDs are verified, the FCU PM task is revised, inspection evidence is required, repeat-defect apartments receive enhanced review, and failure data is monitored for the next 90 days.

Why this example matters

The decision is based on failure evidence and controllable conditions. The target is not an invented savings percentage. The next management question is whether the revised PM actually reduces repeat failures and guest-facing downtime.

Management interpretation

Calculate the cost you can prove first

Maintenance business cases often become weak when they claim large “savings” using unverified assumptions. Start with visible costs: labor, parts, vendor invoices, apartment downtime and recorded recovery. Keep indirect effects separate unless you have defensible data.

For the illustrative HVAC case, if the revised system prevented only four of the 18 comparable incidents in the next 90-day period, the directly avoided parts assumption would be 4 × AED 85 = AED 340, plus 8 labor hours under the scenario assumptions. Whether this creates positive ROI depends on the actual additional PM labor/material cost and any verified downtime or recovery avoided.

Implementation

A practical 30-day PM reset

  1. Days 1–5: validate the asset register and identify critical assets.
  2. Days 6–10: map manufacturer/approved requirements, current PM tasks and known failure history.
  3. Days 11–15: correct task content, frequency, ownership, evidence and escalation rules.
  4. Days 16–20: clean the open backlog and link defects to assets and originating PM where possible.
  5. Days 21–25: establish a small KPI set and daily/weekly exception review.
  6. Days 26–30: audit a sample of completed PMs for quality, not just closure status, and establish the next review date.

B02 management checklist

Before calling the PM system controlled

  • Every critical asset has a unique identity and accountable owner.
  • Criticality logic is documented and consistently applied.
  • PM tasks and frequencies have an approved basis.
  • Work-order closure requires meaningful evidence.
  • Defects found during PM enter controlled corrective work.
  • Critical overdue PM and backlog have escalation rules.
  • PM compliance is reviewed alongside failures and repeat defects.
  • Frequency/task changes are based on evidence and controlled revision.
  • AI tools receive only approved data and do not set technical or safety requirements autonomously.
