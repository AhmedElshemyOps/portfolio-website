#The opening decision: investigate before we invent

The first deliverable in tourism product development should not be an itinerary. It should be a decision statement. A route, price, duration and list of attractions can make an idea look advanced while the underlying opportunity remains untested.

The working question for this project is deliberately wider: Does Amsterdam have an evidence-supported opportunity for a differentiated guided or self-guided tourism experience that visitors value, an operator can deliver commercially, and the destination can accept responsibly?

The answer may be a small-group neighbourhood experience, architecture walk, family product, premium orientation, evening format, cultural programme, day excursion or digital journey. It may also be do not build. The research must be able to reject the initial idea without being treated as a failed project.

This distinction matters in Amsterdam. The City encourages creative and sustainable entrepreneurship that contributes positively to residents while seeking to control visitor pressure. Its Tourism Vision 2035 emphasizes quality of life, responsible revenue models, respectful visitors, smaller groups, cultural value, a polycentric city and regional dispersal. A product can therefore show customer demand and still fail the destination test.

#Amsterdam destination context: growth is not the objective

Official evidence makes the strategic tension clear. Amsterdam Research and Statistics reported 23.7 million tourist overnight stays in 2025 and projected 25.0 to 29.4 million by 2028 across its scenarios. Its 2025 neighbourhood carrying-capacity work reported pressure in six neighbourhoods. CBS separately reported that Amsterdam welcomed 9.5 million accommodation guests in 2025 and recorded the largest percentage increase in overnight stays among the major Dutch cities.

These figures are not interchangeable. Guests, overnight stays, day visits and cruise visits describe different units; datasets may cover different accommodation types, periods and revisions. Their value here is not to prove demand for a new tour. They show that the destination already manages significant volume and that product discovery must examine where, when and how value is created.

The municipal position is more specific than a sustainability statement. Amsterdam seeks products that contribute positively to city life, better visitor dispersal, a richer image based on culture and identity, and responsible entrepreneurship. The national NBTC Perspective 2030 similarly asks tourism to balance benefits and burdens, improve accessibility, support sustainability and contribute to prosperity and well-being. NBTC is developing Perspective 2040, due in 2027, so the national policy layer must be treated as evolving.

For this project, destination fit means measurable compatibility with those directions. Moving a crowded route into a residential area is not automatically dispersal. Calling a place hidden can accelerate pressure. Using local culture without local participation is not cultural integrity. The discovery process must test those risks before launch.

#The business problem and the discovery problem

The business problem is uncertainty: an operator may see strong Amsterdam demand but not know which visitor problem is underserved, which format can differentiate responsibly, or whether the economics and supplier system can support delivery.

The discovery problem is evidence design. Public OTA pages reveal listed products, prices, duration, ratings, review counts and positioning, but not private bookings, impressions, conversion, cancellation rates, ranking logic or negotiated commission. Municipal data describes destination conditions but not willingness to buy one concept. Interviews reveal meaning but not automatically market size. Supplier conversations reveal feasibility but may contain commercial bias.

No single dataset can answer the product decision. The research architecture must combine sources without pretending they measure the same thing.

#The research questions

  • Strategy: what outcomes should Amsterdam and affected neighbourhoods gain or protect?
  • Market: what products and substitutes are publicly available, and where is supply concentrated?
  • Customer: which jobs, pains, desired outcomes and trade-offs appear consistently?
  • Segment: which behaviours, needs and operating languages materially change design?
  • Place: which locations support interest, mobility, accessibility and local value without unacceptable pressure?
  • Operations: which capacities, skills, suppliers, cut-offs and contingencies determine feasibility?
  • Commercial: which cost, price, distribution and capacity combinations survive conservative scenarios?
  • Responsibility: which rules, public-space effects and stakeholder conditions define permission to proceed?

#Research design and sampling plan

The target population is not all Amsterdam tourists. It includes visitors plausibly facing the jobs under investigation, products competing for the same time and budget, and stakeholders affected by delivery. Sampling will be staged so early learning improves later instruments.

StreamInitial sampleInclusion / exclusionDecision use
Competitive products60–100 public listingsBookable Amsterdam-area experiences; remove duplicates/inactive pagesMap structure, not bookings
Reviews/VOC400–600 reviews stratified by category, rating and recencyEnough context to code; exclude empty or likely copied textBuild and validate themes
Visitor interviews15–25, then assess saturationRecent/current visitors; avoid praise-only recruitmentUnderstand jobs and trade-offs
Professional interviews10–15 guides, operators, hotels, attractions and suppliersDirect operating knowledge; separate promotionTest feasibility
Destination stakeholders6–10 local/community/destination actorsAffected or influential participantsTest benefit and harm
Route observationsWeekday/weekend and different conditionsCandidate routes; reject one ideal walk as proofObserve access, flow and pressure

These are starting ranges, not claims of statistical representativeness. OTA and review samples will be stratified so one platform, category, language or rating does not dominate. Interview recruitment will record channel and refusal patterns. Saturation means additional interviews no longer add decision-relevant themes; it does not mean reaching a convenient number.

#Primary and secondary evidence

Secondary research establishes official strategy, public supply, listed pricing, transport, attractions, geography and existing visitor evidence. It should begin with the City of Amsterdam, Amsterdam Research and Statistics, Data Amsterdam, CBS, NBTC, official transport and attraction sources, then original product pages. Weak SEO summaries should not replace a source that owns the data.

Primary research is required where public data cannot reveal the decision. Visitor interviews explore context and trade-offs. Guide and operator interviews test delivery. Supplier conversations validate capacity and terms. Stakeholder interviews examine local value and externalities. Concept tests compare propositions without presenting one concept as inevitable.

Primary evidence will be consented, purpose-limited and minimized. The project does not need passport numbers, booking references or precise home addresses. Recordings require explicit permission and a retention rule. Reports use anonymous participant IDs and avoid combinations that could identify a person.

#Data architecture: preserve observation to decision

The working pipeline is RAW → STAGING → CLEAN → ANALYTICAL → DECISION.

  1. RAW: immutable capture with source, URL, timestamp and original value.
  2. STAGING: imported records with structural checks; no silent correction.
  3. CLEAN: standardized currency, duration, category and missing-value conventions while retaining the original.
  4. ANALYTICAL: derived fields, coded themes, scores and model inputs with formulas and versions.
  5. DECISION: a traceable conclusion linked to evidence, confidence and limitation.

The Tourism Product Data Dictionary defines the minimum schema: record ID, source owner, source URL, observation date, collection date, method, original value, cleaned value, transformation note, missing-data flag, evidence class, confidence, lawful-use note and reviewer.

Raw observations are never overwritten. A listed price changing from €45 to €49 creates a new observation, not an edit. A category correction is logged. An opportunity score points to component records and model version.

Each claim will be classified as Observed Data, Derived Metric, Assumption, Hypothesis or Example. Confidence describes evidence strength; it does not convert a proxy into fact.

#Data quality, limitations and stop rules

Prices vary by date and participant type. Reviews contain selection bias. Product pages may be duplicated. Language availability can change. Statistics may be revised. Geographic data does not automatically reveal resident sentiment. Interviews overrepresent people willing to speak.

The project will monitor completeness, validity, consistency, uniqueness, timeliness and provenance. It will stop or narrow analysis when a definition is missing, platform terms do not support the collection method, one source dominates the sample, privacy cannot be protected, or a conclusion requires unavailable private data.

Review count will never be presented as booking volume. OTA rank will not be called demand. Correlation will not be called causation. Low competition will not become a market gap until customer need, feasibility and destination value are supported.

#Research ethics and platform responsibility

This project will not automatically scrape commercial platforms. Before automation, the team must inspect terms, robots guidance, public APIs, licensed exports and proportionate manual alternatives. Manual structured sampling is the default when automated collection is questionable.

Participants need a clear purpose, voluntary participation, data-use explanation, retention period and withdrawal route. Sensitive information is excluded unless essential and lawful. AI tools receive de-identified inputs; confidential rates, contact details and raw recordings stay outside unapproved systems.

The human researcher owns the taxonomy, checks AI-assisted coding, reviews disagreement and makes the decision. AI may accelerate organization; it does not provide consent, source authority or approval.

#Stakeholder map: influence, interest and value exchanged

The initial map includes visitors, guides, operators, attractions, suppliers, hotels, DMCs, local businesses, residents/community groups, the municipality, destination organizations and mobility providers.

High-influence/high-interest stakeholders should help define conditions, not merely receive a finished concept. Groups experiencing public-space effects require deliberate access. Document the value exchanged, including visitor insight, supplier knowledge, local protection and fair commercial participation.

#How we should visualize this decision

  • Research architecture flow: evidence streams feeding five controlled data layers. It tests traceability; a neat flow must not imply equal source reliability.
  • Source hierarchy: stepped levels from official policy to primary research. It clarifies ownership; a high level is not authoritative for every question.
  • Hypothesis–evidence matrix: hypotheses by evidence stream. Empty cells mean untested, not rejected.
  • Influence × interest map: participants positioned by influence and impact. It supports engagement but must be reviewed, not treated as permanent.

#Copy-ready AI prompts for controlled research

Prompt 01

#Audit the research brief

Use before approving collection.

Purpose: identify missing decisions and controls. Required input: brief, strategy extracts, methods. Expected output: gap table. Human verification: product owner checks scope and sources.

Copy-ready prompt

#Research brief audit

ROLE
Act as a tourism product research governance reviewer.

DECISION/TASK
Audit whether this Amsterdam discovery brief is ready for data collection.

CONTEXT
Balance visitor value, commercial viability, operational feasibility and destination value.

INPUT
[Paste the brief, official strategy extracts, hypotheses, methods and constraints.]

METHOD
Map every decision question to evidence, source authority, method, limitation and owner.

CONSTRAINTS
Use only supplied information. Do not invent demand, regulation, supplier availability or private OTA metrics. Separate fact, assumption and hypothesis.

OUTPUT FORMAT
Return blocking gaps, evidence matrix, ethics/privacy risks, recommended sequence and approval questions.

VERIFICATION
List claims requiring human source checks and the approving role.

Prompt 02

#Review the data dictionary

Use before combining sources.

Purpose: expose incompatible definitions. Required input: field list and sample. Expected output: rules and conflict log. Human verification: analyst approves transformations.

Copy-ready prompt

#Dictionary quality review

ROLE
Act as a tourism data-quality analyst.

DECISION/TASK
Review whether the supplied dictionary preserves auditable Amsterdam research.

INPUT
[Paste field definitions, source notes and de-identified sample records.]

METHOD
Check completeness, type, unit, allowed values, missing rule, original-versus-clean separation, time basis, geography, provenance and transformation trace.

CONSTRAINTS
Do not fill missing values or assume similarly named fields share a definition. Do not convert reviews, ranks or availability into bookings.

OUTPUT FORMAT
Return a field issue log, validation rules, incompatible definitions and human checklist.

VERIFICATION
Identify transformations requiring approval against raw observations.

Prompt 03

#Challenge the hypothesis register

Use to reduce confirmation bias.

Purpose: design falsifiable tests. Required input: hypotheses and evidence. Expected output: disconfirming evidence and tests. Human verification: product lead owns status.

Copy-ready prompt

#Hypothesis challenge

ROLE
Act as a critical tourism product-discovery reviewer.

DECISION/TASK
Challenge each hypothesis and design the cheapest ethical test that could reject it.

INPUT
[Paste the hypothesis register and evidence.]

METHOD
Identify support, disconfirming evidence, proxy weakness, test, threshold and destination implication.

CONSTRAINTS
Use only supplied evidence. Do not reward the preferred concept. Keep UNTESTED where evidence is absent.

OUTPUT FORMAT
Return hypothesis, confidence, challenge, next test, owner and consequence.

VERIFICATION
Explain what a human must check before changing status.

#Research brief and artifact pack

The first pack is controlled templates, not a fabricated dataset:

  • Research Brief — decision, scope, stakeholders, evidence and approval.
  • Hypothesis Register — tests, evidence, status and implication.
  • Assumption Register — confidence, risk, validation and owner.
  • Tourism Product Data Dictionary — definitions and provenance controls.

All four are marked AWAITING DATA. They define collection; they do not pretend it has happened.

#What we know now

  • Amsterdam requires quality of life, responsible entrepreneurship, dispersal and local value alongside demand.
  • Official evidence shows high and potentially rising visitor pressure; that is context, not product demand.
  • Public, primary, commercial and operational evidence must be combined without confusing units or authority.
  • The project needs an auditable pipeline and stop rules before collection.

#What we still do not know

We do not yet know which visitor job is underserved, which segment values it, which route is acceptable, what suppliers can deliver, what price supports the model, which channel fits, or whether destination benefit outweighs burden.

#Assumptions updated

A01–A05 remain OPEN. Official strategy strengthens the requirement for destination evidence but validates no product format.

#Hypotheses updated

H01–H06 remain UNTESTED. None has been promoted based only on general visitor volume or strategic alignment.

#Product decision

AUTHORIZE RESEARCH — DO NOT AUTHORIZE PRODUCT DESIGN. The problem is relevant and the evidence ecosystem is sufficient to begin compliant discovery. The concept remains open, and do not build remains valid.
