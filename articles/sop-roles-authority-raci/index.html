#The operating problem

RACI helps expose responsibility gaps, but a professional SOP must go further. It must identify decision rights, financial limits, communication authority, deputies, shift handoffs, and escalation conditions. Tourism work makes this discipline unusually important. A single service may join Sales, Reservations, Operations, a hotel, an airport team, a transport partner, a guide, a client, and the guest. Information changes while the service is live, and the person who notices a problem may not hold the authority to change cost, timing, supplier, or customer promise. The document therefore has to work as an operating interface, not simply as well-written office content. The starting point is the broader system described in the operating-system overview. That system separates governance, execution, evidence, measurement, and improvement. The present article focuses on one controlled component, while the document-type comparison explains why an SOP, work instruction, checklist, form, template, and record should not be asked to perform the same job. This is a practical management model, not a universal template imposed by ISO or a substitute for applicable law, contract terms, safety requirements, privacy advice, or emergency arrangements. An organization should tailor the depth, approval, terminology, and retained evidence to its services, risk, size, jurisdiction, technology, and competence. Approval should therefore be evidence-led. Reviewers should see the mapped work, representative records, relevant risk and guest requirements, user-test observations, and unresolved issues—not only the polished draft. That evidence makes the approval decision explainable and gives the owner a baseline for measuring whether the released control improves real performance.

#The distinctions that prevent document failure

Responsible is the role doing or coordinating the work. Accountable is the role answerable for the result and approval. Consulted roles provide input before action; informed roles receive relevant information after or during the event. A RACI row should describe an outcome or meaningful activity. If rows are every mouse click, the matrix becomes unreadable; if a row says only ‘operations’, it is too broad to expose ownership. Accountability does not automatically grant unlimited authority. A duty manager may own service recovery while needing commercial approval above a defined additional-cost threshold. Communication responsibility is separate from decision authority. One manager may approve the alternative coach while an assigned guest-facing role communicates the verified plan using the approved message.

The table is intentionally compact. Supporting detail should live in the appropriate controlled document or system rather than expanding one tool until it becomes impossible to use. During review, mark assumptions, unresolved authority, weak inputs, and missing evidence separately; do not convert them into confident statements merely to finish the template.

#A professional design workflow

#List operational outcomes

Begin with the process map: validate booking, approve assignment, confirm readiness, control live movement, authorize recovery, communicate with the guest, close the record.

#Assign responsibility to roles

Use role titles that exist on the roster and organization chart. Avoid writing ‘team’ where one person must act, and define the handoff when responsibility passes between shifts or locations.

#Set one accountable owner where practical

Multiple A entries often mean the decision has not been resolved. When governance genuinely requires joint approval, state the rule and response time rather than hiding it inside a matrix.

#Define consultation with a deadline

Consulted does not mean every stakeholder comments on everything. Identify the question they answer, channel, and latest useful response time so consultation does not paralyze live operations.

#Add authority limits

For cost, cancellation, complimentary service, supplier replacement, data release, and public communication, document what each operational level may authorize and what must be escalated.

#Test deputies and absence

Night shifts, weekends, overlapping groups, and remote partners expose governance that works only when named managers are present. Define acting authority and record how the transfer is acknowledged. After completing the workflow, test the connected design during a delayed coach movement involving operations, the hotel, transport supplier, guide, client, and guest communications. Ask users to work from the draft and approved tools without whispered instructions. Any dependency on a particular manager's memory should be recorded, owned, and resolved through the correct control—document, system, authority, contract, resource, or competence—before release.

#Example: Coordinating a delayed coach movement

Illustrative scenario

Operational teaching scenario, not a documented incident. Uncited figures and outcomes are illustrative assumptions.

Situation

A delayed coach movement requires clear ownership across the hotel, transport supplier, operations and guest communication.

Evidence

Check the approved service brief, current passenger count, confirmed vehicle and driver, access conditions, deadlines and activation authority.

Decision

#Decision

The Duty Operations Manager is Accountable for the service response and can approve a replacement within the agreed commercial limit. Higher cost or cancellation goes to the Operations Director.

Action

#Trigger

A coach reports a mechanical warning forty minutes before a hotel departure. The supplier is Responsible for technical assessment; the Movement Controller is Responsible for coordination.

#Consultation

The hotel desk provides loading status and holding options; the supplier provides replacement ETA; the client account lead is consulted if the change affects the programme.

#Communication

The Guide receives the approved operational message and updates guests. No one speculates about technical causes or promises compensation without authority.

#Common failure modes—and the design response

  • Assigning raci letters to departments that do not recognize the task. Use a recent case to show the operational consequence and identify the earliest control that could have prevented or detected it.
  • Placing more than one unexplained accountable owner on every row. Trace the issue across roles and handoffs; the visible error may originate in an earlier input, authority decision, or system rule.
  • Confusing ‘consulted’ with permission to delay a live decision. Decide whether the remedy belongs in this document, a supporting tool, the system, the contract, resource planning, or competence development.
  • Omitting deputies and shift transfer. Test the proposed correction in a normal case and an exception so that solving one failure does not create another downstream.
  • Failing to separate operational, commercial, and communication authority. Assign an owner and evidence for the correction, then set a date to confirm that the failure has not simply moved into an unofficial workaround.
  • Publishing a matrix inconsistent with job descriptions or contracts. Where risk remains, record and escalate it explicitly rather than adding vague cautionary prose that no user can apply under pressure.

#Implementation and control

  1. Facilitate the first matrix with all affected roles. Confirm the mandate, affected processes, and release decision so ownership does not disappear after the workshop.
  2. Pair raci with an authority-and-escalation schedule. Schedule the work around live operations and include every shift, location, or partner needed to validate the design.
  3. Validate role names against rosters and partner agreements. Keep an issue log with decision owner and due date; unresolved assumptions should never be disguised as approved instructions.
  4. Include raci scenarios in training. Define the evidence of readiness—approved content, system access, supporting tools, briefed partners, competent users, and supervisor cover.
  5. Control matrix changes with the sop revision. Set an early-use review and a routine review trigger, then route defects, competence gaps, and resource problems to different actions.

Implementation should be staged. Begin with a representative service, observe use, and correct design defects before large-scale release. Control the approved version through document control and version management, and use training, competency, and SOP adoption to distinguish a distribution list from genuine competence and adoption.

Do not read one indicator in isolation. A perfect completion rate alongside rising clarification calls may indicate retrospective box-ticking. A temporary increase in reported exceptions can be healthy after training if staff have begun recording issues that were previously hidden. Pair compliance, outcome, user-friction, guest, and improvement signals.

Verification

#Closure

The controller records the decision, approval, communications, revised departure, and supplier follow-up. Finance and the next shift are informed through the controlled record.

The scenario should be validated against the service's current risk assessment and guest journey, not treated as a perfect script. Compare it with the methods in operational risk assessment before SOP writing and customer journey mapping for SOP design. Those articles help the team check whether an internally neat design also addresses operational uncertainty and the guest's visible experience.

Reconcile the physical count with the movement record, confirm the receiving team’s handover and keep unresolved passengers or tasks open.

#Field review before approval

#Boundary

Does the document begin and end at points the team can identify, or does it absorb adjacent processes until no owner can validate it? State the trigger, completion condition, excluded work, and connected processes. Then follow one real job reference from start to finish and note every point where the draft crosses its own boundary.

#Roles and authority

Can every action and decision be traced to a current role, deputy, authority level, and escalation path across shifts and partners? Compare the draft with rosters, job descriptions, delegation limits, and partner contacts. Ask the night or weekend shift who actually decides when the named manager is unavailable.

#Time and sequence

Are triggers, deadlines, intervals, pause points, and handoffs measurable in the systems and working conditions actually used? Use timestamps from a normal case and a disrupted case. Replace vague timing with an observable event or controlled threshold, and confirm that the relevant system or record can capture it.

#Information quality

Are required inputs current, traceable, appropriately protected, and presented in a form that the receiving role can use without reinterpretation? Trace each critical value to its source, owner, format, and latest valid version. Resolve duplicate fields and define what the receiver does when two approved-looking sources disagree.

#Exceptions

Has the team tested delay, absence, conflicting information, technology failure, supplier failure, accessibility needs, and a changing guest situation? Run at least one scenario that exceeds front-line authority. Observe whether staff can recognize the trigger, stabilize the service, escalate with the right facts, communicate, and return the case to controlled closure.

#Evidence

Does the record capture timely facts, source, action, authority, and outcome without unnecessary personal data, speculation, or retrospective reconstruction? Reconstruct a recent event using only retained records. Missing links reveal where the design needs a prompt, automatic timestamp, approval trace, attachment rule, or clearer close-out responsibility.

#Guest experience

Does the control protect the guest's clarity, dignity, accessibility, time, and communication needs as well as the company's internal workflow? Walk through what the guest sees, hears, waits for, and must do. Check language, channel, physical access, assistance, uncertainty, and recovery rather than assuming internal compliance equals a good experience.

#Usability

Can representative users perform the work on the actual device or paper format, under interruption and time pressure, without unofficial notes? Watch rather than coach. Record where users pause, scroll, search, abbreviate, take screenshots, or switch to personal tools; those behaviors are design evidence, not merely user resistance.

#Partner interface

Do external providers understand the same requirement, status, contact, evidence, and change rules, including the right to raise a concern early? Ask the partner to explain the handoff in their own words and demonstrate the communication route. Resolve differences before release, especially around substitutions, delay, cost, guest contact, and retained evidence.

#Learning and review

Will operational data, user feedback, complaints, audits, and change triggers lead to a controlled revision rather than a parallel workaround? Name who reviews which signals, how often, and what action threshold applies. Connect urgent correction, routine revision, refresher training, supplier action, and formal improvement to distinct governance routes.

#Manager's release checklist

  • The purpose, boundary, users, owner, and approval authority are explicit.
  • The content matches the approved process map, risk assessment, customer journey, and current systems.
  • Roles, deputies, thresholds, evidence, privacy, accessibility, supplier interfaces, and exceptions have been tested.
  • Supporting SOPs, work instructions, checklists, forms, templates, records, and training do not contradict one another.
  • Representative users have completed a normal and exception scenario in the actual work environment.
  • The release, access, version, change, retention, review, and withdrawal controls are ready before go-live.
