Yamazumi Chart: How to Balance Workloads and Reduce Waste in Tourism Operations

A practical Lean Management guide for DMCs, tour operators, excursion teams, hotels and event departments.

A busy team is not necessarily a balanced team

Imagine a DMC preparing three excursions during a 60-minute morning window.

The Coordinator must validate manifests, brief guides and resolve supplier issues. The Reservations Officer confirms bookings and guest information. The Dispatch Officer checks transport readiness.

All three employees are busy, but the Coordinator has 68 minutes of assigned tasks. The other employees have 42 and 46 minutes.

The department has 180 minutes of theoretical staff capacity, but that does not mean the work can finish on time. One person is overloaded, and several activities depend on other tasks being completed first.

A Yamazumi chart helps make this problem visible.

In this guide, we explain how to read a Yamazumi chart, investigate Muda, Mura and Muri, calculate the potential benefits of improvement, and use AI to support an evidence-based solution.

The DMC case study uses fictional but operationally realistic numbers. The traditional manufacturing example is supported by published Lean references.

What is a Yamazumi chart?

A Yamazumi chart is a stacked bar chart that displays work elements by employee, role or workstation.

Each bar shows the total assigned task time. The colored sections show the tasks or categories that make up that total.

A horizontal line helps the team compare work content against a meaningful limit.

In traditional production systems, this line is often takt time, calculated from available production time and customer demand.

For the DMC example, the line is a 55-minute workload planning limit within a 60-minute preparation window, with five minutes reserved for minor disruptions. It is not takt time.

Why workload balancing matters in tourism

Tourism companies coordinate several activities that must happen in the correct order.

A booking may need to be confirmed before a guest manifest is finalized. A guide may need an approved manifest before briefing passengers. A driver may need the final pickup instructions before departure.

This creates a connected workflow.

When one role receives too much work, the whole process may slow down, even when other employees have spare capacity.

A Yamazumi chart is useful because it encourages managers to investigate the design of work rather than immediately blaming an individual employee.

However, the chart alone does not show the timing and dependencies of every activity. It should be combined with a task sequence or process map.

Understanding Muda, Mura and Muri

Muda: Waste

Muda is work that consumes resources without creating necessary customer value.

In a DMC, potential waste may include repeated entry of guest details, avoidable searching for documents, unnecessary handoffs, and rework caused by incorrect information.

For example, entering the same approved guest details into three separate systems may be unnecessary if a secure and reliable integration can provide the information.

The team must first establish whether each activity serves a necessary operational, safety, legal or financial purpose.

Mura: Unevenness

Mura refers to irregularity or unevenness.

In tourism, this can occur when multiple excursions depart close together, supplier confirmations arrive in batches, or urgent changes accumulate shortly before departure.

A team may appear to have sufficient total capacity while still experiencing periods of severe pressure.

Muri: Overburden

Muri means overburden.

An employee with 68 minutes of assigned work inside a 60-minute window may be experiencing Muri.

The solution is not necessarily to work faster. The manager should investigate duplicate activities, unclear responsibilities, task dependencies and available support.

These three Lean concepts are connected. Uneven demand can create overburden, while overburden can contribute to mistakes and rework.

Case study: Three excursions in 60 minutes

Our fictional DMC has three roles.

RoleEssential workSupport workDuplicate workTotal
Coordinator34201468
Reservations2414442
Dispatch2816246
Total865020156

All values are minutes.

Figure 1: Current-state Yamazumi chart

The chart reveals three findings.

First, the Coordinator is assigned 68 minutes of work, exceeding the 55-minute planning limit by 13 minutes.

Second, Reservations and Dispatch are below the planning limit.

Third, the department has 20 minutes of work classified as potentially duplicate.

These findings are starting points for investigation, not automatic instructions to remove or transfer tasks.

How overloaded is the Coordinator?

Coordinator utilization against the available hour:

68 ÷ 60 × 100 = 113.3%

Reservations utilization:

42 ÷ 60 × 100 = 70.0%

Dispatch utilization:

46 ÷ 60 × 100 = 76.7%

Total assigned workload:

156 minutes

Total theoretical capacity:

3 × 60 = 180 minutes

Average team utilization:

156 ÷ 180 × 100 = 86.7%

The average hides the problem. The department appears to have spare capacity, yet the Coordinator cannot fit the assigned work into the available hour.

Investigating the duplicate work

The team examines the 20 minutes of potentially duplicate activity.

It discovers that guest information is being copied into multiple files and that some transport details are entered again after being confirmed.

Before changing the workflow, the team checks why these steps exist.

Some information may be repeated because separate departments need approved records. Others may represent unnecessary manual copying.

The improvement should remove avoidable repetition without weakening controls.

Proposed future state

The team proposes removing the 20 minutes of duplicate work.

It also transfers six minutes of necessary support tasks away from the Coordinator.

Four minutes move to Reservations and two minutes move to Dispatch.

During the redesign, the team adds two minutes of necessary validation work to maintain the reliability of the shared information process.

The resulting workload is:

RoleEssential workSupport workDuplicate workTotal
Coordinator3414048
Reservations2418042
Dispatch2820048
Total86520138

Figure 2: Proposed future-state Yamazumi chart

The Coordinator now has 48 minutes of assigned work.

Reservations has 42 minutes.

Dispatch has 48 minutes.

All three roles are below the 55-minute planning limit.

The work has not simply been shifted from one overloaded employee to another. The proposal removes unnecessary duplication and introduces a small amount of necessary validation work.

Figure 3: Before-and-after workload comparison

The chart illustrates the proposed changes by role.

Calculating the improvement

Current total task time:

156 minutes

Proposed total task time:

138 minutes

Net reduction:

156 − 138 = 18 minutes

Percentage reduction:

18 ÷ 156 × 100 = 11.5%

Why is the saving 18 minutes rather than 20 minutes?

Because the proposal removes 20 minutes of duplicate work but adds two minutes of necessary validation.

This is an important Lean lesson: a sound improvement does not eliminate every activity that appears unnecessary. It preserves the controls required to deliver a reliable service.

Does the improvement guarantee on-time departures?

No.

The proposed workload is below the planning limit, but task sequence still matters.

For example, the Coordinator cannot approve the final departure manifest before all required booking updates have been received.

A process map should confirm that critical tasks can be completed in the correct order.

The team should also test the process during late supplier changes, booking amendments and other disruptions.

Financial example: What is the released capacity worth?

Assume the DMC operates 20 comparable preparation sessions per month.

The proposed saving is 18 minutes per session.

Monthly released capacity:

18 × 20 = 360 minutes

360 ÷ 60 = 6 staff-hours

Assume a fully loaded illustrative labor cost of €25 per hour.

Equivalent monthly capacity value:

6 × €25 = €150

Equivalent annual capacity value:

€150 × 12 = €1,800

This is not automatically a financial saving.

The value becomes a realized benefit only when the released capacity produces a measurable result, such as reduced paid overtime, avoided additional staffing, or more productive work.

The team should also monitor errors, delays, guest complaints and employee workload.

A second example: Hotel group check-in

Consider a fictional hotel preparing for the arrival of a tour group.

Three employees share the preparation tasks.

RoleAssigned work
Receptionist A52 minutes
Receptionist B31 minutes
Guest Relations27 minutes

The preparation window is 45 minutes.

Receptionist A exceeds the available time by seven minutes.

The hotel reviews room-list validation, welcome materials, group documentation and guest communication.

The team discovers that some welcome materials can be prepared earlier and that certain repeated checks may be replaced with a controlled shared record.

The lesson is not to eliminate guest checks. It is to organize the work so that essential checks happen reliably without unnecessary repetition.

A Yamazumi chart can help visualize the workload, but the team must still validate the proposed changes in actual operations.

A documented manufacturing example

The Lean Enterprise Institute describes operator balance charts as tools for distributing work elements in relation to takt time.

Its published discussion of an AIO assembly cell illustrates how work elements can be stacked and compared with takt time to reveal imbalance.

This is the traditional manufacturing application from which the tourism adaptation draws its principles.

The manufacturing example and the fictional DMC example should be clearly distinguished. The DMC figures are educational calculations, not results from the AIO facility.

Use AI to solve the problem, not just draw the chart

AI can help organize task records, check arithmetic, investigate possible causes and prepare a controlled improvement proposal.

However, it cannot independently know how long employees take to perform tasks without reliable input data.

It also cannot determine whether an employee is qualified to perform a transferred task unless that information is provided.

Master Prompt 1: Diagnose and redesign the workload

Copy this complete prompt:

Act as a senior Lean Management and operational excellence consultant.

Help me analyze and improve a real operational process using a Yamazumi chart, Muda, Mura, Muri, root-cause analysis and a controlled improvement plan.

First, understand the process. Ask for the industry, department, process boundaries, roles, available time, customer demand, task list, observed durations, task sequence, dependencies and required controls.

Ask only essential missing questions. Do not invent data.

Validate all records, units, calculations and assumptions. Separate measured information from estimates.

Calculate total workload by role and prepare a stacked-bar chart dataset. Distinguish takt time from a deadline-based planning limit.

Identify potential Muda, Mura and Muri. Explain the evidence and uncertainty for each finding.

Investigate likely causes using an appropriate method, such as 5 Whys or process mapping. Do not present unverified hypotheses as confirmed causes.

Propose a future-state workflow that preserves safety, quality, legal requirements, privacy, employee competence and operational deadlines.

Calculate before-and-after workload, potential time savings and capacity impact.

Distinguish theoretical capacity value from realized financial savings.

Identify implementation risks, hidden dependencies and possible new bottlenecks.

Design a controlled pilot with KPIs, success criteria, stop conditions and a rollback plan.

Before producing the final answer, independently recheck all arithmetic and verify that every proposed task transfer is operationally feasible.

Return an executive summary, current-state analysis, chart-ready data, Lean findings, future-state plan, financial evaluation, risk assessment, pilot plan and management decision checklist.

Use clear English and explicitly label every assumption.

Master Prompt 2: Challenge the proposed solution

Act as an independent Lean improvement auditor.

Review the analysis and future-state proposal I provide.

Check all calculations, workload totals, task dependencies, planning limits, operational controls and financial claims.

Look for hidden work, transferred bottlenecks, unrealistic assumptions, missing approvals, insufficient training and risks to service quality.

Classify important findings as supported, plausible but unverified, incorrect, or requiring more data.

Explain exactly what must be corrected or measured.

Finish with one recommendation: ready for controlled pilot, revise before pilot, or insufficient evidence.

Do not approve implementation merely because the proposed chart looks balanced.

Master Prompt 3: Build the implementation plan

Act as an experienced Lean implementation manager.

Using the validated analysis and proposed future state, prepare a practical pilot plan.

Define responsibilities, training, process changes, required approvals, communication arrangements and risk controls.

Create a KPI table showing each metric, formula, data source, measurement frequency and responsible owner.

Include workload, departure readiness, accuracy, rework, overtime, employee feedback and customer impact.

Recommend pilot success criteria, stop conditions and a rollback procedure.

Clearly distinguish proposed targets from targets supported by actual baseline data.

Finish with a management review template and a decision checklist.

Do not recommend permanent SOP changes until pilot results have been evaluated and approved.

Filter every AI recommendation

Before applying an AI-generated improvement, ask five questions.

Evidence: Is the recommendation supported by observations?

Accuracy: Are the numbers and formulas correct?

Feasibility: Can the proposed tasks be completed in the required order and by qualified employees?

Risk: Are safety, guest service, privacy and operational controls protected?

Value: Will the change deliver a measurable benefit without moving the problem elsewhere?

If a recommendation fails an important check, investigate or revise it before testing.

Review the measures together.

A faster process is not necessarily better if it increases mistakes or creates excessive pressure.

Seven-day improvement pilot

Day 1: Select the process and define the problem.

Day 2: Observe the work and collect representative task data.

Day 3: Build the current-state Yamazumi chart.

Day 4: Investigate Muda, Mura and Muri.

Day 5: Design and risk-assess the proposed future state.

Day 6: Conduct a supervised test with suitable backup arrangements.

Day 7: Review the results and decide whether further testing is required.

Some operations will need longer observation and testing periods. Seven days is only an illustrative starting framework.

Common mistakes to avoid

Do not treat estimates as measured data.

Do not use Yamazumi charts to rank employees.

Do not assume that every employee should have an identical workload.

Do not ignore task timing and dependencies.

Do not remove essential safety or quality checks.

Do not claim financial savings when only theoretical capacity has been released.

Do not implement a future state without employee involvement, risk review and testing.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Yamazumi chart?

A stacked bar chart showing task time by employee, role or workstation, helping teams investigate workload balance.

What is the difference between Muda, Mura and Muri?

Muda means waste, Mura means unevenness, and Muri means overburden.

Can tourism companies use Yamazumi charts?

Yes. They can adapt the principles to excursion preparation, reservations, dispatch, hotel operations and event coordination.

Can AI create a Yamazumi chart?

Yes, when reliable task data is supplied. AI can prepare chart-ready information and support analysis, but operational decisions require human verification.

Is the 55-minute planning limit takt time?

No. It is an illustrative workload limit within a 60-minute preparation window. Takt time requires available production time and customer demand.

Does removing 18 minutes of work guarantee financial savings?

No. It releases potential capacity. Realized savings must be demonstrated through actual operational or financial outcomes.

Conclusion

A Yamazumi chart can reveal problems that are difficult to see when everyone appears busy.

In our fictional DMC example, one coordinator had more assigned work than could fit into the preparation window, while other employees had additional capacity.

The chart helped identify a potential workload imbalance and duplicate activities.

The proposed future state reduced assigned task time from 156 to 138 minutes, a modeled improvement of 11.5%.

The improvement would still need to be tested against real deadlines, operational controls, service quality and employee workload.

AI can help managers analyze the information and design better solutions, but it must not replace observation, professional judgment or appropriate approvals.

Make the work visible. Investigate the cause. Test the improvement. Measure the result.

That is how Yamazumi charts can support better tourism operations.
