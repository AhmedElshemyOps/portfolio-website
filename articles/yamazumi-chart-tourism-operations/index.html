Three excursions must depart within one hour. The Reservations Officer checks bookings, Dispatch confirms transport, and the Coordinator validates manifests and briefs guides. Everyone looks busy. Yet one person has 68 minutes of assigned work in a 60-minute window. How can a manager diagnose the problem without rushing employees or weakening guest checks?

A Yamazumi chart provides a visual starting point. This guide explains how to build one, use the Lean concepts of Muda, Mura and Muri, evaluate a proposed redesign and challenge the results using structured AI prompts.

What is a Yamazumi chart?

A Yamazumi chart, also called an operator balance chart, displays task times as stacked segments grouped by worker, role or workstation. A reference line helps compare assigned work with a meaningful capacity limit. In manufacturing the line may be takt time, calculated from available production time and customer demand. Here, the line is a 55-minute planning target within a 60-minute preparation window, reserving five minutes for minor disruptions. It is not takt time.

Three Lean problems: Muda, Mura and Muri

Muda — avoidable waste

Repeatedly copying guest information between booking records and manifests may be unnecessary. But an independent final verification may be a required control. Investigate the purpose of each step before removing it. Other potential wastes include searching for missing documents, unnecessary handoffs, corrections and waiting.

Mura — unevenness

Three departures scheduled close together create peaks. A role may be idle earlier and overloaded shortly before the deadline. Total minutes alone cannot reveal all arrival patterns: pair Yamazumi with a process timeline and observe when each task becomes actionable.

Muri — overburden

Assigning 68 minutes of work to one coordinator in a 60-minute window suggests overburden. The manager should review staffing, timing, qualifications and duplicated work before demanding greater speed. The goal is reliable service and sustainable working conditions, not 100% utilization.

Net assigned task time falls from 156 to 138 minutes: 18 ÷ 156 × 100 = 11.5% modeled reduction. The Coordinator falls by 20 minutes; Dispatch increases by two minutes; Reservations stays at 42 minutes. The change combines task removal, redistribution and added validation.

What could 18 minutes be worth?

At 20 comparable preparation sessions per month, the model releases 18 × 20 = 360 minutes, or six staff-hours monthly. At an illustrative fully loaded labor cost of €25/hour, that is €150/month or €1,800/year in equivalent capacity value. It is not automatically a cash saving. Financial benefit requires reduced paid overtime, avoided hiring, increased productive output or another verified outcome. Implementation costs and error rates must also be considered.

Second scenario: hotel group check-in

A fictional hotel prepares for a group arrival in 45 minutes. Receptionist A has 52 minutes of tasks, Receptionist B has 31 and Guest Relations has 27. The first role exceeds the window by seven minutes. The hotel may pre-prepare welcome materials, clarify room-list ownership and eliminate unnecessary copying. But it must retain identity, payment and room-allocation controls. This illustrates transferability of the method, not a measured hotel result.

Three AI master prompts to diagnose, challenge and implement

Use anonymized, approved task data. Do not paste identifiable guest details, confidential contracts or sensitive employee information into an unapproved AI service. The first prompt asks for missing evidence rather than guessing; the second independently audits the solution; the third converts a validated proposal into a controlled pilot.

1. Diagnose and redesign

Act as a senior Lean operations consultant. Guide me through a Yamazumi workload investigation. First ask for process boundaries, role names, available time, demand, task-level observed durations, sequence, dependencies, qualification requirements, service quality and safety controls. Ask no more than five essential questions at once. Never invent missing values. Separate measured facts, estimates and assumptions. Validate units, duplicates and totals. Calculate role workload, utilization and chart-ready stacked task data. Distinguish takt time from a deadline-based planning target. Identify possible Muda, Mura and Muri with evidence and uncertainty. Use 5 Whys only to develop testable root-cause hypotheses. Propose a feasible future state preserving guest safety, privacy, quality, employee qualifications and approval rights. Recalculate each task reassignment and net change, including any added controls. Distinguish released labor capacity from realized cost savings. Design a controlled pilot with baseline KPIs, acceptance criteria, stop conditions and rollback. Independently recheck all arithmetic. Output a current-state table, proposed-state table, chart data, assumptions, risk register, experiment plan and management decision checklist.

2. Independently audit

Act as an independent Lean improvement auditor. Challenge my proposed Yamazumi redesign. Check every total and percentage; validate task sequence, deadlines, employee skills, approvals, data privacy, quality checks, bottleneck migration, interruptions and the reliability of the measurements. Categorize each finding as evidenced, plausible but unverified, incorrect, or missing data. Identify the precise test or observation needed. Recommend ready for controlled pilot, revise, or insufficient evidence. Do not equate a visually balanced chart with a safe operation.

3. Plan a controlled pilot

Act as a Lean implementation manager. Convert the validated Yamazumi proposal into a controlled operational pilot. Specify owners, task handoffs, required approvals, training, system changes, communications and contingency arrangements. Define workload utilization, on-time readiness, manifest accuracy, rework, overtime, guest impact and employee feedback KPIs, with formulas, sources, frequency and owners. Propose provisional thresholds clearly labeled as assumptions, stop conditions, rollback steps and a management review. Do not recommend permanent SOP changes before evidence-based approval.

Filter AI suggestions before acting

Every recommendation should pass five checks: evidence (measured or assumed?), arithmetic (correct totals and units?), feasibility (skills, timing and dependencies?), risk (guest safety, privacy and quality protected?), and value (measurable benefit without a shifted bottleneck?). A balanced-looking chart does not establish operational safety.

A practical seven-day starting pilot

Day 1: Define the process and the problem. Day 2: Observe and record task times. Day 3: Create the current-state chart. Day 4: Investigate waste, unevenness and overburden. Day 5: Design and risk-assess a future state. Day 6: Test under supervision with a rollback option. Day 7: Review evidence and decide whether further testing is needed. Longer pilots may be necessary for seasonal or variable demand.

Frequently asked questions

Is Yamazumi the same as a Gantt chart?

No. Yamazumi shows work content and workload by role. A Gantt chart shows activities against a timeline. They complement each other.

Should all employees have identical workload?

No. Assignments must respect qualifications, authority, task sequence, variation and contingency capacity.

Can AI make a Yamazumi chart?

Yes, from supplied task data. AI can calculate and visualize a model, but observations and operational review are required to validate it.

Does an 11.5% task-time reduction guarantee savings?

No. It is a modeled capacity release. Actual benefits must be measured.

Conclusion

A Yamazumi chart helps managers ask a better question than “Why is this person slow?”: “How is the work designed, and where does the system create unnecessary pressure?” In the fictional DMC case, visualizing task times reveals a possible bottleneck, and the proposed redesign reduces assigned time while preserving essential checks. The next step is to test the idea with real observations, not to assume the chart proves success.

Make the work visible. Investigate the cause. Test the improvement. Measure the result.
