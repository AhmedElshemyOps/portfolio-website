#The operating problem

A professional STP is a decision instrument. It converts a requirement into an observable challenge and produces evidence that supports release, correction, or rejection. The document must be executable by another competent person without relying on the author's memory. Tourism operations are unusually exposed at interfaces. Sales may define the promise, Reservations holds changing information, Operations coordinates capacity and decisions, suppliers provide critical resources, field teams face the guest, and Finance or the client needs evidence afterward. A control that works inside one department can still fail at the handoff, during a change, or outside office hours. The method in this article is therefore designed as part of an end-to-end operating system. The wider architecture is explained in the tourism-company operating system, while the document-type comparison separates policies, SOPs, work instructions, checklists, forms, templates, and records. That distinction matters because a management method should generate the right controls and evidence rather than forcing every requirement into one document. This is a practical interpretation for tourism management, not a claim that any cited framework prescribes the exact template shown here. The organization remains responsible for tailoring terminology, depth, approval, safety, privacy, employment, contractual, financial, accessibility, regulatory, emergency, and records controls to its jurisdiction, services, risks, technology, and competence.

#Concepts that must stay distinct

Every STP starts with a test objective linked to a requirement, risk, change, complaint pattern, audit finding, or recovery capability. A broad objective such as ‘check airport operations’ cannot establish meaningful coverage or pass criteria. Preconditions are controlled facts that must exist before Step 1: process and system versions, roles, test data, configuration, supplier participation, environment, permissions, equipment, and safety controls. Unrecorded preconditions make a pass difficult to reproduce. Expected results describe observable predicted behavior; actual results describe what was produced or observed. Combining them in one narrative encourages hindsight and hides small deviations that may expose a larger control weakness. A failed step is not automatically a failed person. The cause may sit in information, design, system logic, workload, supplier interface, training, authority, or the test itself. Defect classification should lead to investigation, not blame.

#Practical tool: Tourism STP template with traceability, defect, and approval fields

Use the following canvas in a working session with a current process, representative service records, and the people who perform or receive the work. Mark assumptions and missing evidence openly; a blank or disputed answer is more useful than a confident invention.

ElementManagement questionTourism application
ObjectiveWhat must the test establish?Backup transport can be authorized and mobilized within the recovery threshold
System or processWhich controlled version is in scope?After-hours recovery SOP v2.1 and dispatch configuration 6.4
PreconditionsWhat must be true before execution?Duty roster, backup agreement, test data, contacts, permissions, stop criteria

The canvas is intentionally compact. Its output should link to the controlled source—process map, risk register, requirement, data definition, SOP, system rule, supplier interface, training record, test, dashboard, audit, or improvement case. Copying detail into several places creates the appearance of completeness while weakening change control.

#A professional method

#1. Create traceability

Assign an STP identifier and link the objective to its requirement, risk, process, SOP version, system configuration, and change record. This gives reviewers a reason for the test and a defined scope.

#2. Write precise preconditions

List data, resources, roles, authorizations, supplier readiness, starting states, and environmental limits. State how each precondition is confirmed and what invalidates the run.

#3. Design representative test data

Use realistic but appropriately protected booking, guest, flight, vehicle, contact, timing, and pricing data. Include boundary values and conflicting or missing inputs where they represent credible operating risk.

#4. Write atomic steps

Give each step one clear action, actor where needed, input, and sequence. Put the expected result beside the step that should produce it so evidence and deviation are located quickly.

#5. Define severity and stop rules

Agree which outcomes are minor, major, or critical; who may pause or terminate the test; what must be contained; and which failures block release.

#6. Execute without coaching the process

The tester follows the approved procedure and captures actual results, timestamps, evidence, and deviations. Unplanned help is recorded because it may prove the design cannot stand on its own.

#7. Review, correct, and retest

A reviewer checks completeness and independence, defects receive owners and due dates, corrective changes follow control, and failed or affected steps are repeated before closure.

At the end of the method, require a decision backed by evidence: proceed, proceed conditionally with named controls, return for correction, or stop. Record the decision maker, date, scope, residual issues, and next review. Ambiguous approval is especially dangerous in tourism because live teams and partners will fill the gap differently under time pressure.

#Running example: Validating an after-hours service-recovery process when the primary transport supplier cannot respond

#Test objective

Prove that the duty team can recognize primary-supplier non-response, authorize backup within delegated limits, mobilize the alternative, control guest communication, and retain evidence.

#Test data

A fictional late-night airport service is created with a valid booking, inaccessible primary dispatcher, approved backup rate, guest contact preference, and a connection-sensitive arrival time.

#Execution

The controller triggers the escalation, provides the defined information pack, obtains approval, confirms the backup asset, updates the movement record, and issues the approved message.

#Observed defect

The backup supplier receives the pickup time but not the terminal meeting point. The actual result fails the completeness criterion even though the vehicle could still be mobilized.

#Closure

The dispatch template and interface checklist are corrected, the same case is rerun by a different controller, and a second scenario checks that the new mandatory field does not obstruct hotel-departure recovery.

The example is not a universal SOP. A company should repeat the reasoning with its own service promise, systems, suppliers, volumes, guest profiles, legal environment, and disrupted conditions. Compare the design with operational risk assessment and customer-journey mapping for SOP design so operational control, risk, and the visible guest journey remain connected.

#Common failure modes—and the management response

  • Leaving the objective unlinked to a requirement. Trace the operational consequence using a recent case, then identify the earliest control that could have prevented, detected, or safely contained the condition.
  • Omitting versions and preconditions. Follow the issue across upstream inputs and downstream handoffs. The visible lapse may originate in workload, information, authority, system design, supplier behavior, or an incentive outside the user's control.
  • Packing several actions into one test step. Decide whether the remedy belongs in process design, the SOP pack, technology, resource planning, supplier governance, competence, or leadership policy. Adding prose to the wrong document will not control the cause.
  • Recording only pass or fail without actual results. Test the proposed remedy in a normal case and a credible exception. Confirm that improvement in one measure has not transferred delay, risk, cost, or confusion to another role or the guest.
  • Letting the author approve their own unexplained deviation. Assign one accountable owner, evidence, due date, and review point. An informal workaround may protect today's service but should not silently become the future operating method.
  • Retesting the fix without checking connected processes. Record the residual risk and escalate it through the approved route. A vague warning such as ‘take care’ is not an observable control and cannot support an effectiveness decision.

#Implementation roadmap

Implementation should be proportionate to risk and change. The same five gates can govern a small improvement or a cross-company launch, but the evidence, independence, resources, testing, and approval depth should match the potential consequence.

#Phase 1: Mandate

Confirm sponsor or owner, scope, authority, success criteria, affected stakeholders, risk, resources, and the evidence required before proceeding. Put unresolved assumptions in an issue log rather than disguising them as approved facts.

#Phase 2: Design

Use observed work, representative records, customer and partner needs, data, risk, and front-line knowledge. Align the method with process maps, SOPs, systems, contracts, supporting tools, roles, and existing governance.

#Phase 3: Pilot

Choose representative normal, boundary, and exception cases. Protect live operations, define acceptance and stop criteria in advance, record actual results, correct defects through control, and retest affected interfaces.

#Phase 4: Release

Verify approved content, access, system readiness, partner acknowledgement, competent users, supervisor coverage, communications, rollback or contingency, monitoring, support, and explicit go-live authority.

#Phase 5: Sustain

Review early use, compare results with baseline, audit deployment and effectiveness, route recurrence through RCA and CAPA, preserve lessons, and control revisions so improvement does not create parallel versions.

Control the approved version through document control and version management. Use training, competency, and SOP adoption to verify competence and adoption rather than counting distribution or attendance. Where suppliers perform part of the process, apply the interface and performance disciplines in supplier and partner governance.

#Measures that support decisions

Select a small balanced set. For each measure define purpose, formula or classification rule, numerator and denominator where applicable, source, owner, refresh, segmentation, target, response threshold, and known limitations. A measure without an action route becomes reporting overhead.

Read the measures together. A better average can hide a damaging tail; a lower complaint count can reflect harder reporting; a perfect checklist score can coexist with weak outcomes; and a rise in recorded exceptions may be healthy when previously hidden problems become visible. Segment results and combine performance data with observation, audit, guest evidence, supplier context, and front-line feedback.

#Field review before approval

#Value and outcome

Can the team state which guest, client, employee, partner, or organizational outcome this method protects, and what evidence would show that value was actually created? Review one successful and one failed service so internal completion is not confused with customer value.

#Boundary and ownership

Are trigger, completion, exclusions, process owner, deputies, and connected services explicit? Follow one service reference end to end and note every point where work crosses a department, system, shift, location, or supplier boundary.

#Risk and resilience

Have credible failure, peak demand, absence, conflicting information, technology loss, supplier failure, accessibility need, and recovery been examined proportionately? Confirm that efficiency does not remove the capacity or discretion needed to stabilize a disrupted service.

#Data and evidence

Are definitions, sources, timestamps, versions, calculations, access, corrections, and retention controlled enough to support the stated decision? Reconstruct an event from records and identify any conclusion that depends on memory, a private message, or an unverified field.

#People and authority

Can representative users explain and demonstrate their role, decision limits, handoffs, and escalation under real working conditions? Include night, weekend, remote, temporary, and partner personnel where they form part of the service.

#Guest and partner interface

Does the design control what external parties need to know, provide, acknowledge, decide, and record without exposing them to contradictory instructions? Walk through the visible guest experience and ask a partner to demonstrate the handoff in their own words.

#Usability and technology

Can the method be used on actual devices, channels, locations, languages, and workload conditions without unofficial notes or coaching? Observe searching, scrolling, duplicate entry, screenshots, manual workarounds, and automation failure as evidence about design.

#Learning and governance

Will incidents, complaints, defects, tests, audits, measures, user feedback, supplier signals, and external change reach a named review and controlled decision? Distinguish urgent correction, formal CAPA, routine revision, project change, and strategic improvement.

#Manager's approval checklist

  • The purpose, service value, scope, owner, users, authority, and required outcome are explicit.
  • The method reflects observed work, current systems, representative data, guest needs, partners, risk, and disrupted conditions.
  • Definitions, roles, thresholds, evidence, privacy, accessibility, safety, exceptions, and escalation are controlled at the appropriate level.
  • Supporting documents, systems, tools, contracts, training, tests, dashboards, and records agree with one another.
  • Representative users and partners have demonstrated the design in normal and exception scenarios without hidden coaching.
  • Release, access, version, support, monitoring, audit, change, retention, improvement, and withdrawal arrangements are ready.
