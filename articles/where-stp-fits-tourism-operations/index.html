#The operating problem

An SOP defines the approved way to operate; an STP defines how to challenge a requirement under stated conditions, compare expected and actual results, record defects, and make a release decision. Tourism companies need both when failure would otherwise be discovered by a live guest. Tourism operations are unusually exposed at interfaces. Sales may define the promise, Reservations holds changing information, Operations coordinates capacity and decisions, suppliers provide critical resources, field teams face the guest, and Finance or the client needs evidence afterward. A control that works inside one department can still fail at the handoff, during a change, or outside office hours. The method in this article is therefore designed as part of an end-to-end operating system. The wider architecture is explained in the tourism-company operating system, while the document-type comparison separates policies, SOPs, work instructions, checklists, forms, templates, and records. That distinction matters because a management method should generate the right controls and evidence rather than forcing every requirement into one document. This is a practical interpretation for tourism management, not a claim that any cited framework prescribes the exact template shown here. The organization remains responsible for tailoring terminology, depth, approval, safety, privacy, employment, contractual, financial, accessibility, regulatory, emergency, and records controls to its jurisdiction, services, risks, technology, and competence.

#Concepts that must stay distinct

Testing is not another word for training. Training builds a person's capability; an STP gathers evidence about a process, system, interface, or contingency. A trained dispatcher may still be working with a broken alert rule, contradictory status codes, or an untested supplier handoff. A walkthrough asks whether the design appears logical. A simulation observes people and interfaces in a realistic scenario. A test applies defined preconditions, steps, expected results, and acceptance criteria. These methods can complement one another, but their evidence is not interchangeable. The STP belongs beside the controlled process pack, not inside every operational SOP. Linking the test identifier, requirement, risk, defect, corrective action, owner, and retest preserves traceability without forcing front-line users to read validation instructions during live service. Release decisions should be risk-based. A spelling change may need document review; a changed dispatch algorithm, emergency contact route, airport meeting point, payment control, or after-hours escalation can justify controlled testing before deployment.

#Practical tool: SOP–STP decision matrix and readiness-test charter

Use the following canvas in a working session with a current process, representative service records, and the people who perform or receive the work. Mark assumptions and missing evidence openly; a blank or disputed answer is more useful than a confident invention.

ElementManagement questionTourism application
Do the workSOP or work instructionPerform the normal transfer assignment and monitoring flow
Confirm critical actionsChecklistVerify vehicle, driver, coordinator, signboard, contacts, and access
Capture factsForm or system recordRecord readiness, status, exception, decision, and closure evidence
Challenge the designSTPChange a flight, remove a resource, or break a communication channel
Evaluate the resultAcceptance criteriaCorrect alert, authorized decision, controlled recovery, complete record
Resolve failureDefect and CAPA routeContain, investigate, correct, retest, approve or reject release

The canvas is intentionally compact. Its output should link to the controlled source—process map, risk register, requirement, data definition, SOP, system rule, supplier interface, training record, test, dashboard, audit, or improvement case. Copying detail into several places creates the appearance of completeness while weakening change control.

#A professional method

#1. Identify what must be proved

Start with a requirement or risk, not a desire to ‘test everything’. State the business consequence if the control fails and the evidence needed for a release decision.

#2. Choose the right verification method

Use review for static content, walkthrough for logic, simulation for coordinated behavior, and an STP when conditions, actions, expected results, and pass/fail evidence must be repeatable.

#3. Set a controlled boundary

Name the process version, systems, roles, suppliers, locations, service type, exclusions, and test environment. A pass outside the intended boundary must not be presented as universal assurance.

#4. Define acceptance before execution

Agree expected results, tolerance, severity rules, evidence, and approval authority before testers see the outcome. This prevents a convenient result from quietly redefining success.

#5. Protect people and live operations

Use safe data, authorized scenarios, rollback arrangements, privacy controls, and explicit stop criteria. Never create guest, worker, asset, or service risk merely to make a test realistic.

#6. Record defects without improvising fixes

Capture the failed step, actual result, evidence, impact, severity, owner, and containment. Route design changes through the correct control so the test record remains an honest account.

#7. Retest and decide

Repeat the failed condition after correction, run regression checks on connected controls, and record who approved release, conditional release, further action, or rejection.

At the end of the method, require a decision backed by evidence: proceed, proceed conditionally with named controls, return for correction, or stop. Record the decision maker, date, scope, residual issues, and next review. Ambiguous approval is especially dangerous in tourism because live teams and partners will fill the gap differently under time pressure.

#Running example: Testing the complete airport-transfer control chain before a high-volume arrival programme

#Requirement

When an inbound flight changes beyond the approved threshold, the movement system must flag affected assignments and route a decision to the duty controller.

#Preconditions

A current manifest, assigned vehicles, representative contacts, approved status codes, and a safe training dataset are loaded into the test environment.

#Challenge

The tester changes one flight, makes the primary coordinator unavailable, and observes system alerts, deputy ownership, reallocation, supplier communication, and guest-message control.

#Evidence

Timestamps, screen records, acknowledgements, decision authority, revised assignments, and the exception log are compared with the expected result and tolerance.

#Decision

A missing deputy alert is classified as a release-blocking defect. The rule and duty roster interface are corrected, the scenario is repeated, and connected arrival waves are regression-tested.

The example is not a universal SOP. A company should repeat the reasoning with its own service promise, systems, suppliers, volumes, guest profiles, legal environment, and disrupted conditions. Compare the design with operational risk assessment and customer-journey mapping for SOP design so operational control, risk, and the visible guest journey remain connected.

#Common failure modes—and the management response

  • Copying the sop and calling it a test. Trace the operational consequence using a recent case, then identify the earliest control that could have prevented, detected, or safely contained the condition.
  • Testing only the normal path. Follow the issue across upstream inputs and downstream handoffs. The visible lapse may originate in workload, information, authority, system design, supplier behavior, or an incentive outside the user's control.
  • Writing expected results after seeing the outcome. Decide whether the remedy belongs in process design, the SOP pack, technology, resource planning, supplier governance, competence, or leadership policy. Adding prose to the wrong document will not control the cause.
  • Using live guest data without controlled need. Test the proposed remedy in a normal case and a credible exception. Confirm that improvement in one measure has not transferred delay, risk, cost, or confusion to another role or the guest.
  • Closing a defect because a workaround exists. Assign one accountable owner, evidence, due date, and review point. An informal workaround may protect today's service but should not silently become the future operating method.
  • Treating one successful run as permanent assurance. Record the residual risk and escalate it through the approved route. A vague warning such as ‘take care’ is not an observable control and cannot support an effectiveness decision.

#Implementation roadmap

Implementation should be proportionate to risk and change. The same five gates can govern a small improvement or a cross-company launch, but the evidence, independence, resources, testing, and approval depth should match the potential consequence.

#Phase 1: Mandate

Confirm sponsor or owner, scope, authority, success criteria, affected stakeholders, risk, resources, and the evidence required before proceeding. Put unresolved assumptions in an issue log rather than disguising them as approved facts.

#Phase 2: Design

Use observed work, representative records, customer and partner needs, data, risk, and front-line knowledge. Align the method with process maps, SOPs, systems, contracts, supporting tools, roles, and existing governance.

#Phase 3: Pilot

Choose representative normal, boundary, and exception cases. Protect live operations, define acceptance and stop criteria in advance, record actual results, correct defects through control, and retest affected interfaces.

#Phase 4: Release

Verify approved content, access, system readiness, partner acknowledgement, competent users, supervisor coverage, communications, rollback or contingency, monitoring, support, and explicit go-live authority.

#Phase 5: Sustain

Review early use, compare results with baseline, audit deployment and effectiveness, route recurrence through RCA and CAPA, preserve lessons, and control revisions so improvement does not create parallel versions.

Control the approved version through document control and version management. Use training, competency, and SOP adoption to verify competence and adoption rather than counting distribution or attendance. Where suppliers perform part of the process, apply the interface and performance disciplines in supplier and partner governance.

Select a small balanced set. For each measure define purpose, formula or classification rule, numerator and denominator where applicable, source, owner, refresh, segmentation, target, response threshold, and known limitations. A measure without an action route becomes reporting overhead.

Read the measures together. A better average can hide a damaging tail; a lower complaint count can reflect harder reporting; a perfect checklist score can coexist with weak outcomes; and a rise in recorded exceptions may be healthy when previously hidden problems become visible. Segment results and combine performance data with observation, audit, guest evidence, supplier context, and front-line feedback.

#Field review before approval

#Value and outcome

Can the team state which guest, client, employee, partner, or organizational outcome this method protects, and what evidence would show that value was actually created? Review one successful and one failed service so internal completion is not confused with customer value.

#Boundary and ownership

Are trigger, completion, exclusions, process owner, deputies, and connected services explicit? Follow one service reference end to end and note every point where work crosses a department, system, shift, location, or supplier boundary.

#Risk and resilience

Have credible failure, peak demand, absence, conflicting information, technology loss, supplier failure, accessibility need, and recovery been examined proportionately? Confirm that efficiency does not remove the capacity or discretion needed to stabilize a disrupted service.

#Data and evidence

Are definitions, sources, timestamps, versions, calculations, access, corrections, and retention controlled enough to support the stated decision? Reconstruct an event from records and identify any conclusion that depends on memory, a private message, or an unverified field.

#People and authority

Can representative users explain and demonstrate their role, decision limits, handoffs, and escalation under real working conditions? Include night, weekend, remote, temporary, and partner personnel where they form part of the service.

#Guest and partner interface

Does the design control what external parties need to know, provide, acknowledge, decide, and record without exposing them to contradictory instructions? Walk through the visible guest experience and ask a partner to demonstrate the handoff in their own words.

#Usability and technology

Can the method be used on actual devices, channels, locations, languages, and workload conditions without unofficial notes or coaching? Observe searching, scrolling, duplicate entry, screenshots, manual workarounds, and automation failure as evidence about design.

#Learning and governance

Will incidents, complaints, defects, tests, audits, measures, user feedback, supplier signals, and external change reach a named review and controlled decision? Distinguish urgent correction, formal CAPA, routine revision, project change, and strategic improvement.

#Manager's approval checklist

  • The purpose, service value, scope, owner, users, authority, and required outcome are explicit.
  • The method reflects observed work, current systems, representative data, guest needs, partners, risk, and disrupted conditions.
  • Definitions, roles, thresholds, evidence, privacy, accessibility, safety, exceptions, and escalation are controlled at the appropriate level.
  • Supporting documents, systems, tools, contracts, training, tests, dashboards, and records agree with one another.
  • Representative users and partners have demonstrated the design in normal and exception scenarios without hidden coaching.
  • Release, access, version, support, monitoring, audit, change, retention, improvement, and withdrawal arrangements are ready.
